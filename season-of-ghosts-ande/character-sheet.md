## Appearance

An aging man with pale blue skin, worn smooth and a little grey at the edges by his years. His eyes are pupilless and pale as small moons, the mark of his samsaran soul. A pair of short horns sit at his brow, and his teeth are a touch too sharp, the gifts of his mother's oni blood, along with faint dark markings at his throat and wrists. His hair is iron grey, his hands scarred and calloused from a lifetime of practice with a blade he has never once drawn in earnest. He dresses plainly. Across his back he carries a nodachi, still wrapped head to foot in cloth and old prayer paper, which he treats less like a weapon and more like something he is guarding.

His face is kind, and his presence is not. People who have known him their whole lives still give him a little room when the wrappings come off the blade.

## Backstory

### Childhood

Zhen came to Willowshore as a small child, brought there by his father. The man was a weathered veteran of Imperial Lung Wa, a career soldier of an empire that still looked mighty in those years even as it rotted quietly from the inside. He had served it long enough to lose his faith in it, in its bloated and arbitrary aristocracy, in the secret police it had loosed to hunt a corruption that only ever seemed to spread, and in the open contempt it held for anything not born human. A man like that, raising a child like Zhen, had every reason to disappear. So he took his strange blue son to the most remote and forgotten corner of the empire he could reach, the neglected backwater of Shenmen, and the two of them came to Willowshore alone, somewhere no imperial eye would ever trouble to look.

The boy was strange from the first. His skin was blue, his eyes were pale and pupilless like small moons, and he had a pair of short horns and teeth that were a little too sharp. His father was never troubled by any of it. He explained all of it as oni blood, for Zhen's mother had been an oni, and she was gone now in a way the old man never spoke about.

The veteran raised the boy himself and taught him the only things a soldier knows how to give. He taught Zhen the blade and the healing hand, the craft of cutting and the craft of mending, and above the two of them he taught discipline. A man who had lived by violence understood exactly where an unchecked hunger leads, and he refused to let his son walk that road. He gave Zhen a nodachi wrapped in cloth and prayer paper, never once mentioning that it had belonged to his mother, and he set a single rule upon it. Keep it always, but never draw it until you are certain you are ready. So a strange blue child in a wary town learned restraint before he learned anything else, from the one man who understood why it mattered.

### Becoming Zhen

When his father passed, the grief opened something in him. The dead began to speak, and he found that he could hear them and hold them, the way an animist carries the spirits he channels. He heard a great many of them in the years that followed. He never once heard his father. The old man had passed on cleanly, the way the rites promise, and that quiet absence became a small private grief that Zhen kept to himself and did not name to anyone.

He took what the spirits gave him and what his father had taught him and made a life out of both. He became the town's healer, and the keeper of its smaller mercies. He grew close to You So-Jin, the herbalist who tends the graves, and he came to love Willowshore's folklore and its old stories, which always felt less like things he was learning and more like things he was remembering. People gave him their wary looks at first. Most of them came around once they knew him, the way a small town eventually does with anyone who stays and gives more than he takes. All the while the hunger grew with him, year after year, and year after year he held it down. The nodachi stayed wrapped.

### Stillness, and now

The fragments came later, as he grew older. He began to remember pieces of a life he had not lived in this body, a warrior who loved the killing, who took to the blade and the blood without shame, who had named himself Glory. The memories matched the hunger in him too closely to be anything but his own. Only then did he understand the full shape of what he was. He carried an oni's appetite in his flesh, and the soul of a man who had already surrendered to it once.

He chose his name then. A samsaran takes a second name for the thing it means to become, and he took Stillness, because the way through was never to run from what lived inside him. The way through was to master it. A true stillness, earned by holding the line against the hunger every single day of this life, was the only road forward worth walking. The hunger grew harder to carry as the years went on, and he grew more patient in answer. In time the town simply called him Old Zhen.

Then came the summer of the festival. When the time came to choose the year's abductees for the Reenactment, Old Zhen put his name forward the way he always had, as one of the town's elders honoring its oldest traditions, and he walked out to the clearing on the edge of town to spend the festival night under the stars with the others. The wrapped nodachi went with him, the way it always did. It had stayed bound for the better part of fifty years, and he still could not say whether he would ever judge himself ready to draw it. He had only ever been certain of one thing about that day, on the rare nights he let himself think of it at all. When it came, it would not be a thing he chose. It would be a thing that was asked of him.
